Ride and roll flags use typical passenger-car ranges. ARB stress flags use acceptance bands of yield FOS 1.5–1.9 and torsional FOS 0.9–1.3. All are guides; replace with your program targets.

1. Masses

Sprung mass is everything carried by the springs: body, battery, occupants. Unsprung mass moves with the wheel: tyre, wheel, hub, brake, knuckle and roughly half of the links, springs and dampers.

Ride comfort is about controlling the sprung mass. Road holding is about keeping the unsprung mass in contact with the road. A heavy EV battery raises sprung mass, which lowers ride frequency for the same springs, so EV springs are usually stiffer.

Corner sprung mass ms = (M × axle share) / 2 − mu

2. Motion ratio and wheel rate

The spring rarely sits at the wheel. The motion ratio (MR) is spring travel divided by wheel travel. Because both force and displacement are scaled, the rate at the wheel scales with MR squared.

Wheel rate kw = ks × MR²

Example: a 75 N/mm spring at MR 0.72 gives only 38.9 N/mm at the wheel. MacPherson struts are near 0.9–1.0; multi-link rears with the spring on the lower arm are often 0.6–0.8.

The tyre is a second spring in series with the suspension. The combined ride rate is always softer than either.

Ride rate kride = kw·kt / (kw + kt)

3. Ride frequency and flat ride

Each corner behaves like a mass on a spring. Its natural frequency is the main comfort number.

f = (1 / 2π) × √(kride / ms)   [k in N/m, m in kg]
  • Comfort cars: 1.0–1.3 Hz. Sporty cars: 1.3–1.6 Hz. Race cars: 2.5 Hz and above.
  • Static deflection is linked directly: δ = ms·g / kride, and f ≈ 15.76 / √δ(mm). Low frequency needs long travel.
  • Flat ride (Olley): the front hits a bump first. If the rear is 10–20% higher in frequency, it catches up and the body bounces instead of pitching. Rear/front ratio of about 1.05–1.20 is the usual aim.

4. Wheel hop

The unsprung mass also oscillates, between the suspension spring and the tyre. This is typically 10–15 Hz. Lower unsprung mass raises it and improves grip on rough roads.

fhop = (1 / 2π) × √((kw + kt) / mu)

5. Damping

The damper removes energy. The damping ratio ζ compares the damping at the wheel with the critical damping, the value that stops oscillation with no overshoot.

cwheel = cdamper × MRd²    ccrit = 2√(kride·ms)    ζ = cwheel / ccrit
  • Passenger cars run about 0.25–0.45 average. Lower feels floaty, higher feels harsh.
  • Rebound controls the body (it acts when the spring pushes the body back up), so it is usually 2–3× bump.
  • Bump controls how much road shock passes into the body. Too much bump damping is a common cause of harshness.
  • This app uses the low-speed coefficient (piston speed below about 0.1 m/s), which governs body control.

6. Roll centre and roll axis

The roll centre is set by the suspension linkage geometry. The body rolls about it. The line joining front and rear roll centres is the roll axis.

The distance h from the CG down to the roll axis is the lever arm the cornering force uses to roll the body. Raising roll centres reduces roll but increases jacking and makes the car react more abruptly.

Roll axis height at CG hra = RCf + (RCr − RCf) × (xcg / L)    h = hcg − hra

7. Roll stiffness

Roll stiffness is the moment needed per degree of roll. Springs, the anti-roll bar and the tyres all contribute.

Springs: Ks = kw × t² / 2    Tyres: Kt = kt × t² / 2
Axle: K = 1 / (1/(Ks + Karb) + 1/Kt)

Track width enters squared, so a wider track is a strong way to reduce roll.

8. Anti-roll bar

The ARB is a torsion bar that only works when the two wheels move in opposite directions. It adds roll stiffness without changing ride frequency in single-wheel-pair bounce.

J = π(d⁴ − di⁴) / 32    Kθ = G·J / Lactive
Karb = Kθ × MRarb² × t² / a² × η
  • Stiffness rises with diameter to the fourth power: +1 mm on a 24 mm bar is about +18%.
  • Shorter lever arm a makes the bar stiffer (1/a²) but raises stress.
  • η (75–90%) accounts for arm bending, bushes and link compliance. Calibrate it from CAE or a rig test when you can.
  • A hollow bar keeps most of the stiffness at much less mass, which is attractive for EVs.

9. Roll gradient

Roll gradient is degrees of body roll per g of lateral acceleration. The denominator includes a small term because the CG shifting sideways itself adds roll moment.

φ / ay = ms·g·h / (Kf + Kr − ms·g·h)   [rad/g → × 57.3 for °/g]

Typical: comfort sedans 3–4 °/g, sporty cars 2–2.5 °/g, SUVs up to 4–5 °/g.

10. Lateral load transfer and balance

In a corner, load moves from the inner to the outer wheels. Tyres lose grip efficiency as load increases, so the axle that takes the larger share of load transfer loses grip first.

ΔFz = (ay·g / t) × [ ms,tot·h·Kaxle/Ktot  +  ms,axle·RC  +  mu,axle·r ]

The three terms are elastic transfer (through springs and bar), geometric transfer (through the linkage, set by roll centre height) and unsprung transfer. More front share pushes the car toward understeer; more rear share toward oversteer. Passenger cars usually sit a few percent above their front weight share for stable understeer.

11. ARB stress check

At the design roll angle φ, the bar twists by θ. Torsion gives shear; the offset between the arm and the bush adds bending. The combined maximum principal stress is compared to yield.

θ = MRarb·φ·t / a    T = Kθ·θ·η    τ = T·(d/2) / J
F = T / a    M = F·e    σb = M·(d/2) / (J/2)
σ1 = σb/2 + √((σb/2)² + τ²)    FOSy = Sy/σ1    FOSτ = 0.577·Sy/τ

This is a hand check for sizing. Confirm stress concentration at bends and the fatigue life with CAE and rig testing.

Good working order

  1. Fix mass, CG and axle split for the load case.
  2. Choose ride frequencies and the front/rear ratio, then size springs from the motion ratios.
  3. Set the roll gradient target and the front share of roll stiffness, then size the ARBs to fill the gap left by the springs.
  4. Check load transfer balance and inner-wheel load at the maximum lateral g.
  5. Set damping ratios, then check ARB stress at the design roll angle.
  6. Verify on CAE and vehicle, then tune η and the model from test data.
